## Summary
This paper introduces PRISM, a neural framework for vehicle routing problems (VRPs) that learns a continuous, typed edge-level value—termed "constraint-interaction fields"—to rank feasible moves within a feasibility-guaranteed action space. Unlike prior multi-task solvers that condition on variant identifiers or unified data representations, PRISM employs a resource-token attention mechanism where each active constraint (capacity, time windows, backhauls, etc.) is described by an algebraic descriptor. This allows a single trained policy to transfer zero-shot across 110 different constraint compositions. The method is trained via decision-level Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and integrated into a local-search refinement loop. Empirically, PRISM demonstrates significant gap reductions against a distance-only ablation and outperforms the current state-of-the-art unified solver (URS) on heavily constrained, multi-depot, and asymmetric routing variants.

## Strengths
**1. Clear Problem Framing and Compositional Generalization:** The paper correctly identifies a fundamental limitation in current neural VRP solvers: the inability of binary feasibility masks to capture the continuous, state-dependent value of moves under interacting constraints. The shift toward learning a "value" rather than a "policy per configuration" is a meaningful conceptual contribution. The use of a typed algebraic descriptor for resources (Section 5.3) is a clever mechanism to achieve zero-shot transfer to unseen constraint combinations without expanding the model parameter count.

**2. Rigorous Ablation Design:** The experimental setup is notably clean. By initializing all learned terms in Equation (1) to exactly zero, the authors ensure that the "Field-off" control is a byte-identical instance of the same search algorithm with $E(e) = c(e)$. As noted in Proposition 1 and Section 7.6, this paired protocol isolates the contribution of the learned signal from the underlying search mechanics (the decoder and refinement loop), providing high confidence that the observed improvements are indeed attributable to the learned fields.

**3. Impressive Empirical Breadth:** Evaluating across 110 constraint compositions is a substantial undertaking. The results in **Table 2** show that the mean gap reduction is actually larger on held-out compositions (3.35 pp) than on seen ones (1.81 pp). This strongly supports the central hypothesis that the network learns a generalizable interaction algebra rather than memorizing per-variant heuristics. Furthermore, **Table 3** highlights that as constraints stack (e.g., moving from CVRP to AMDCVRPLTW), the performance margin between PRISM and the baseline URS widens dramatically, confirming that the method excels precisely where the problem complexity increases.

## Weaknesses
**1. The "Value vs. Penalty" Semantics and Mathematical Formulation:** The authors insistently frame Equation (1) as a "learned value" and explicitly deny it is a "penalty method." However, mathematically, $E(e \mid s) = c(e) + \sum_{r} \lambda_{r}(s) [\text{field}_{r}(e) + a_{r}(e)]$ is structurally identical to a Lagrangian relaxation or a penalized objective where $\lambda_r$ are dual variables/multipliers. The argument that "feasibility is already guaranteed... so the $\lambda_r$ enforce nothing" is logically shaky. If $\lambda_r$ scales the contribution of a resource's field to the total energy, and the optimizer minimizes that energy, $\lambda_r$ absolutely enforces a preference—it pushes the search away from states where the weighted resource cost is high. Calling it "pure value" instead of a "penalty" does not change the optimization dynamics; it merely reflects that the penalties never need to diverge to infinity because the hard decoder acts as a safety net. This terminological obfuscation makes it difficult to position the work against the vast literature on Lagrangian duality and Augmented Lagrangian methods in combinatorial optimization.

**2. Unfair Comparison Against Construction-Only Baselines (URS):** A significant portion of the paper's impact claim rests on **Table 3**, comparing PRISM against URS. However, URS is a single-pass autoregressive construction policy, whereas PRISM drives a full Scope-Restricted Refinement (SRR) local-search loop. The authors acknowledge this in Section 7.1, stating "the URS margin reflects both the refinement search and the field," yet they still lead with the aggregate 19.5% vs 1.3% comparison. Looking closely at **Table 3**, on simple compositions like `cvrp` or `acvrpl`, URS actually outperforms PRISM (or PRISM's field-off control). This implies that the "advantage" of PRISM on complex stacks might largely be attributable to the inclusion of *any* iterative refinement (even the distance-only version of PRISM beats URS on average), rather than the novelty of the learned fields. To truly validate the field's contribution, the authors should compare against URS augmented with a standard classical local search (e.g., LKH-3 or HGS) used as a post-processing step, which is the standard deployment mode for such solvers.

**3. Over-Engineering and Lack of Parsimony:** The architecture described in Section 6 and Appendix C is remarkably complex. It involves a graph encoder, resource-token attention, a state coupler, multiple auxiliary heads (dual loss, binding classifier, continuation-risk), and a specialized "field-gated exploration" budget. While the ablation in **Table 5** attempts to justify some of this, the sheer number of moving parts (e.g., the specific choice of 32-dimensional descriptors, the tanh scaling, the inverse decision-count weighting in PPO) raises concerns about overfitting to the benchmark's specific instance distribution. Is the compositional transfer robust, or is it a result of a highly tuned system that happens to work on this specific set of 110 variants? The lack of comparison against simpler baselines (e.g., a standard GNN-predicted edge cost without the full "algebraic descriptor" machinery) makes the minimal viable innovation unclear.

**4. Ambiguity in the "Resource Algebra" Generalization Claims:** Section 5.3 claims the registry is "open" and that new resources like fuel or emissions can be declared through the same 32-dimensional descriptor. However, the paper provides no empirical evidence for this. All 110 compositions are built from the exact same 10 pre-defined families. There is no experiment showing a user adding an 11th resource family (e.g., a stochastic travel time or a driver rest-period constraint) and seeing successful zero-shot transfer. Without this, the claim of an "open registry" remains theoretical speculation. The "algebra" might simply be a fixed embedding layer for 10 known constraint types.

**5. Reliance on Heuristic References and Absolute Gap Interpretation:** The paper reports gaps relative to "reference solutions" produced by various oracles (LKH-3, OR-Tools, etc.). In Section 7.1, the authors note that these references are not optimal and that solvers can sometimes beat them (negative gaps). While the *paired reduction* is invariant to reference quality, the absolute levels presented in **Table 6** are difficult to interpret. For example, on `amdcvrp`, URS reports a 48.02% gap while PRISM reports 2.21%. Such a massive discrepancy often suggests either a failure of the baseline on that specific instance type or that the reference solution for that specific composition is exceptionally weak. Aggregating these vastly different reference qualities into a single mean gap (as done in Table 3) can be statistically misleading.

## Potentially Missing Related Work
- **Bi et al. (2024)**, *Learning to Handle Complex Constraints for Vehicle Routing Problems*: Proposes a Proactive Infeasibility Prevention (PIP) framework for explicit constraint awareness. This is directly relevant as it addresses how neural solvers handle complex, interacting constraints beyond simple masking, and should be discussed in Section 2 and Section 4 as an alternative approach to the "mask vs. value" dichotomy.
- **Corrêa et al. (2026)**, *FiLMMeD: Feature-wise Linear Modulation for Cross-Problem Multi-Depot Vehicle Routing*: Introduces a unified model for 24 multi-depot VRP variants using feature-wise linear modulation conditioned on active constraints. This is a direct competitor in the "unified/compositional" space and should be added to **Table 1** and compared empirically, especially given PRISM's emphasis on multi-depot performance.
- **Jari et al. (2025)**, *G-UniRouting: A Graph-Based Unified Neural Model for Solving Multi-Attribute Vehicle Routing Problems*: Presents a graph-based unified model for multi-attribute VRPs. This work targets the same goal of generalizing across multiple real-world VRP variants and should be cited in the related work section to distinguish PRISM's value-field approach from graph-unification approaches.
- **Liu et al. (2026)**, *A Generic Neural Solver for Vehicle Routing Problems*: Develops a generic neural decision framework for cross-problem generalization. This is a highly relevant recent work that shares the motivation of a single model for many VRP variants and should be included in the discussion of Section 7.
- **Canoy et al. (2023)**, *Learn and route: learning implicit preferences for vehicle routing*: Learns implicit preferences to reshape route quality. This aligns with PRISM's goal of augmenting base costs with learned signals and should be referenced when introducing the concept of the "move-value" in Section 4.

## Questions
1. **Regarding the "Value vs. Penalty" distinction:** If the learned intensities $\lambda_r(s)$ were to become very large during training, wouldn't the optimization effectively behave exactly like a penalized Lagrangian method? Have you analyzed the magnitude/range of these multipliers during inference to verify they remain in a "value" regime rather than a "constraint-enforcement" regime?
2. **Regarding the URS comparison:** Would you consider adding a baseline where URS solutions are refined by a standard, non-learned local search (like the SRR you use but driven by distance)? This would help the community understand if the gain comes from the *neural field* or simply from the *presence of any refinement loop*.
3. **Regarding the Open Registry:** Since all experiments use the same 10 resource families, can you provide any proof-of-concept (even on synthetic data) where a genuinely novel resource type (not seen in the 10 families) is added to the descriptor and the model transfers successfully?
4. **Regarding Computational Cost:** You mention the network is queried 7-9 times per composition. How does the wall-clock inference time of PRISM compare to URS and to classical solvers like LKH-3 on these instances? For a practitioner, is the quality improvement worth the increased computational budget?

## Contribution Rating
3: Good
*Justification: The shift toward compositional, algebraically-conditioned values for VRP is a valuable direction. The empirical coverage of 110 variants is a significant practical contribution. However, the incremental nature of the improvement over a "distance-only refinement" baseline and the lack of comparison to other recent generic/unified solvers (FiLMMeD, G-UniRouting) slightly tempers the perceived impact.*

## Overall Rating
6: Marginally above the acceptance threshold. But would not mind if paper is rejected.
*Justification: PRISM presents a solid, well-engineered framework for compositional vehicle routing with impressive empirical results on complex constraint stacks. The "typed field" concept is a legitimate contribution to representation learning in combinatorial optimization. However, the paper suffers from an unfair comparison against a construction-only baseline (URS) without a refinement-augmented control, and the mathematical formulation of the "learned value" is arguably a repackaging of existing penalty/Lagrangian ideas with a hard-decoder safety net. The exclusion of several recent relevant works on unified/generic VRP solvers further limits the confidence in its positioning. It is a strong paper that would benefit from a more honest appraisal of its advantages versus classical refinement and a broader baseline comparison.*
